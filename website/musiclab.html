BUT WAIT. DIFFERENT PEOPLE LIKE DIFFERENT THINGS.

How different is different?

Even without a crowd to follow, two groups won’t agree perfectly. So the researchers split the independent group into comparable halves and measured how far their song shares drifted apart.

THE RECORD KEEPS TWO IDEAS AT ONCE

Appeal matters.
So does the path.

Independent demand is positively associated with social success. But the same song can land in very different places across worlds. An audience doesn’t just discover success; the information it sees can help shape it.

Random assignment makes the groups comparable in expectation. Separate worlds let different social histories unfold. Splitting the independent group gives us a benchmark for differences without visible popularity.

This does not show that talent is irrelevant or hits are purely random. Downloads alone cannot tell us whether people followed the crowd, treated popularity as a quality signal, or changed their tastes. Independent demand is an appeal benchmark, not objective musical quality.

KEEP THE LINER NOTES

Behind the results

The reproduction agrees on direction. One threshold doesn’t match.

The original supplement reports comparison fractions below .01 for both first-experiment results. Our 1,000-split run gives 0.01075 for inequality and 0.004071 for unpredictability.

A documented 10,000-split run with a separate fixed seed gives 0.012225 and 0.003721, respectively. Using the 721-person half for Gini gives 0.012675. The inequality threshold does not exactly reproduce; its direction does. The cause is unresolved. No seed searching was used to cross a threshold.

These implement the authors’ particular comparison: among all observed social-statistic/control-split pairs, what fraction has social lower than control? They are not conventional null-randomization or t-test p-values, probabilities the null is true, or confidence intervals for a causal effect.

What the causal design can—and can’t—tell us

The intervention is seeing prior downloads from your own world. Both conditions had the same songs, individually randomized menu positions and listen–rate–optional-download sequence. Randomization supports comparable tastes in expectation, not identical realized tastes. Interaction within worlds is intended; information leaking between worlds would threaten the comparison.

The analysis uses repeated partitions of the same independent records, not 1,000 independent experiments. The eight social worlds provide eight histories; the 28 overlapping pairs are dependent. HHI and coefficient of variation also show greater concentration in the prepared analysis.

These were free downloads of unfamiliar songs in a particular population and interface. This is a reproduction of the paper’s first experiment, not a full-paper replication, a new participant experiment, or proof of how Spotify works.

Data checks, ranks and replay limits

All nine worlds’ per-song totals reconcile across participant records and timestamped download indicators. Three timestamped rows without a download indicator were not counted. There are 29 download pairs and 36 rating pairs without matching recorded listens; their cause is unknown. We do not invent missing listens.

Released records already reflect exclusions for possible contamination. Unavailable excluded raw data prevent a complete exclusion audit. The release prints 6,626 social downloads in one descriptive table; the released records reconcile to 6,625.

Our competition rank is 1 plus the number of songs with strictly more downloads. Tied songs share a rank; song ID only stabilizes row order. Original tie handling is unspecified. Shares use each world’s download total, because world sizes differ.

Replay uses 68 calendar-day snapshots from 7 October to 13 December 2004 plus an empty initial state. Shares, ranks and Gini are undefined at zero downloads. No within-day order or original visible-counter reconstruction is claimed.
